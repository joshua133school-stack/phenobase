1. Why record phenology

Phenology means the timing of the stages a plant goes through each year: buds opening, flowering, fruit ripening, leaves falling. You already watch these stages on your farm. When you write down the dates every year, in the same way as other farmers, the records become very valuable:

  • You can compare this year with past years and with other farms in your region.
  • Extension services can give better warnings of frost at flowering and better advice on harvest timing.
  • Researchers can see how climate change is affecting crops in Asia, where very little such data exists today.

Recording takes a few minutes per visit. A few years of careful dates from one farm are worth more than many careless ones.

2. What you need

  • This guideline and the recording sheet (printed), or a phone with the online form.
  • Coloured ribbon, tape or labels to mark your trees or plot.
  • A pen. Keep the sheet in a plastic bag in the orchard or field.
  • A phone camera (optional, but very helpful).

3. Choose your plants

3.1 Fruit trees

  1. Choose one cultivar (for example 'Fuji' apple or 'Niitaka' pear). If you grow several, start with the main one.
  2. Choose 5 healthy trees of normal size and age for your orchard. Do not choose trees at the edge of the orchard, next to buildings or walls, or trees that are sick or newly planted.
  3. Mark the 5 trees with ribbon and number them 1 to 5. Observe the same trees every year.
  4. If a tree dies or is removed, choose a similar tree nearby and write this in the notes.

3.2 Rice, cereals and other field crops

  1. In the middle of the field, at least 5 steps from the edge, mark a fixed plot: about 10 plants, or about 1 square metre of rice or wheat.
  2. Mark the corners with sticks. Use the same field every year if you can; the plot is re-marked each season.
  3. Write the sowing date and, for rice, the transplanting date.

3.3 Describe your site once

On the first sheet, write: village, province, country, cultivar, age of the trees, and whether the crop is in the open field, under a rain shelter or in a greenhouse. If you have a phone, use the "Use my location" button on the online form while standing in the orchard or field.

4. When and how often to look

  • During fast-changing periods (from bud burst to the end of flowering, during heading, and near harvest), look every day or every second day.
  • At other times, look once a week.
  • Look at about the same time of day, preferably late morning when flowers are open.
  • Look at the whole tree or plot, on the sunny side and the shady side, not only at one branch.

5. How to decide the date

The date of a stage is the first day that the stage is true for your marked plants as a group.

If the stage name says…It means
FirstYou can see it on at least one of your marked plants.
BeginningAbout 1 in 10 buds, flowers or plants.
Full or halfAbout half (5 in 10) or more.
EndAlmost all (9 in 10 or more).
Harvest maturityThe day you would normally start the main harvest.

Rules

  1. Write the date as year-month-day, for example 2027-04-21. If you use the lunar calendar, convert to the solar calendar.
  2. If you missed the day (for example you were away), write your best estimate, mark "how sure" as L (low), and write in the notes the last day you saw "not yet" and the first day you saw "already".
  3. If you did not see a stage at all, leave it blank. Never guess without saying so, and never copy a neighbour's date.
  4. Record only what you saw on your marked plants, even if the rest of the orchard or field is earlier or later.
  5. The stars (*) mark core stages. Please always record these. The other stages are welcome but optional.

6. Stages: deciduous fruit trees

Apple, pear, peach, ume, apricot, cherry, plum, grape, persimmon, jujube, chestnut, kiwifruit, blueberry.

CodeStageWhat you see
07*Bud burst (green tip)Bud scales open and green leaf tips show on about 1 in 10 buds.
11First leaves unfoldedThe first leaves have fully opened out of the bud.
60*First flowers openThe first fully open flower on any marked tree.
61Beginning of floweringAbout 1 in 10 flowers are open.
65*Full floweringHalf or more of the flowers are open. The first petals may be falling.
69*End of floweringAlmost all petals have fallen.
71Fruit setYoung fruits are clearly swelling (about the size of a fingernail, 10 mm).
81Beginning of ripeningFruits begin to colour or soften.
87*Harvest maturityThe fruit is ready for the main harvest.
93*Beginning of leaf fallAbout 1 in 10 leaves have changed colour and fallen.
97End of leaf fallAll or almost all leaves have fallen.

Grape: "flowers open" means the cap (calyptra) falls off the flower. Ume and apricot often flower before bud burst; record each stage when it happens, in any order.

7. Stages: evergreen and tropical fruit trees

Citrus (satsuma mandarin, yuzu), loquat, lychee, longan, mango, durian, rambutan, mangosteen.

CodeStageWhat you see
51*Flower buds / panicles visibleFlower buds or flower panicles can be seen on about 1 in 10 shoots.
60*First flowers openThe first fully open flower on any marked tree.
65*Full floweringHalf or more of the flowers are open.
69*End of floweringAlmost all flowers have finished.
71Fruit setYoung fruits are clearly swelling.
81Beginning of ripeningFruits begin to change to their ripe colour or the skin changes.
87*Harvest maturityThe fruit is ready for the main harvest.

Flower induction: if you use a chemical or other treatment to make trees flower (common for mango, longan and durian), write the date and the product in the notes. This is very important for understanding the dates. If trees flower twice in one year, record each flowering separately and write "second flowering" in the notes.

8. Stages: rice and other cereals

Rice, wheat, barley, maize, foxtail millet.

CodeStageWhat you see
00*SowingThe day you sowed the seed (seedbed or field).
09*EmergenceSeedlings show above the soil or water in about half of the plot.
21Tillering beginsThe first side shoot (tiller) is visible on about half of the plants.
51*Heading beginsTips of ears or panicles come out of the leaf sheath on about 1 in 10 plants.
55*Heading dateEars or panicles have come out on about half of the plants.
65Full floweringSmall anthers (pollen sacs) hang out of about half of the ears or panicles.
89*Fully ripeGrain is hard and your thumbnail cannot dent it. Ready to harvest.
99HarvestedThe day you harvested.

Rice transplanting: write the transplanting date in the notes of the "09 Emergence" or "00 Sowing" line. If you grow two or three rice crops a year, use a new sheet for each season and write the season name (for example "dry season").

9. Stages: vegetables, legumes and root crops

Soybean, adzuki bean, mung bean, peanut, sesame, perilla, chili pepper, napa cabbage, radish, garlic, onion, sweet potato, potato.

CodeStageWhat you see
00*Sowing / plantingThe day you sowed or planted (seed, seedling, clove or tuber).
09*EmergenceShoots show above the soil in about half of the plot.
41Harvested part begins to formCabbage leaves start to fold into a head; bulbs, roots or tubers start to swell.
49*Harvested part matureHead, bulb, root or tuber has its normal harvest size and firmness.
51Flower buds visibleFlower buds can be seen on about half of the plants.
60*First flowers openThe first open flower in the plot.
65Full floweringAbout half of the plants have open flowers.
71First fruits / pods setFirst fruits or pods have their typical shape on about half of the plants.
81Beginning of ripeningFirst fruits change colour or first pods turn yellow or brown.
89*Fully ripeFruits or pods are fully ripe and ready for the main harvest.
99HarvestedThe day you harvested.

For crops harvested for leaves, heads, bulbs or roots (cabbage, radish, garlic, onion, potato, sweet potato), the core stages are 00, 09 and 49. For crops harvested for seed or fruit (beans, sesame, pepper), they are 00, 09, 60 and 89.

10. Other things to write down

Write these in the notes column with the date. They help explain early or late years.

  • Frost, hail, typhoon, strong wind, very hot days, drought, flood. Give the damage as none, light or heavy.
  • Pruning, flower thinning, fruit thinning, flower induction treatment.
  • Greenhouse, rain shelter, heating, frost fans or sprinklers.
  • Serious pest or disease attack.
  • Anything unusual, for example flowering again in autumn.

11. Photographs

  • Photographs help the national coordinator check the stage. They are not required.
  • Take one photo of the whole tree or plot and one close-up of a typical branch or plant.
  • Stand in the same place each time, so photos from different days can be compared.
  • Do not include people's faces.

12. Your data and your rights

  • Your observations belong to you. You decide to share them, and you can ask for them to be removed at any time.
  • Your name, phone number and exact address are kept only by your national coordinator and are never published. In the database you appear only as an observer code, for example OBS-KR-017.
  • The location of your farm is published only to about 1 km.
  • Each season you will receive your own records back, together with the average for your region.
