We are developing a community-supported crop phenology observation network across Asia. Our work focuses on making field observations more accessible and consistent, particularly in agricultural communities that have limited involvement in existing scientific reporting programs.

Rather than establishing a separate research database, we aim to supply existing projects with reliable, high-resolution observations and the raw data behind them. Our own database serves primarily to organize, verify, and distribute contributed records.

1. Limitations

Limited participation and coverage. Many agricultural communities remain underrepresented in organized phenology reporting. Existing platforms may be unfamiliar or difficult to access, particularly for older growers and people in rural areas. Language barriers and limited opportunities for training can make participation even less practical.

Inconsistent reporting. Phenological observations collected by different contributors can be difficult to compare. Growth stages may be interpreted differently, and records often lack information about the crop variety or cultivation conditions. Isolated observations also provide less information than repeated records of the same plants throughout a growing season.

Loss of original information. Phenology data are often distributed as processed measurements, estimated transition dates, or aggregated results. These outputs are useful, but without the underlying observations, researchers may be unable to verify classifications, assess uncertainty, or reprocess records using different methods. Original photographs and environmental measurements are especially valuable as image-analysis methods and phenological models improve.

Environmental uncertainty. Weather records from distant stations or coarse-resolution climate products may not accurately represent conditions at an individual growing site. This becomes particularly important when comparing crop development across different cultivation environments.

2. Solutions

Our first priority is outreach. We aim to work directly with growers, particularly in rural communities, using local-language guidance and practical assistance to introduce phenology reporting. Participation should not require previous scientific experience.

We are developing crop-specific observation manuals and a simplified recording application, alongside custom scanning and photographic tools that contributors will be able to request. These are intended to reduce differences in how observations are made and recorded.

Contributors will be encouraged to monitor the same plants or plots repeatedly throughout a cultivation cycle. Each record will identify the crop and its growth stage, supported by dated photographs and information about the cultivar and growing conditions. Observation procedures will follow standardized definitions wherever appropriate.

Preserving and sharing raw data is a central part of our approach. Original photographs, field observations, and available environmental measurements will be retained alongside standardized records. Corrections and derived measurements will remain distinguishable from the original submissions, allowing researchers to revisit the source material instead of relying solely on processed results.

We also aim to improve the environmental context of observations by incorporating reliable local weather measurements where available. Other meteorological sources will be documented so that researchers can account for differences in spatial resolution and measurement uncertainty.

Our internal database will support quality checks and data exchange with established research platforms. We intend to explore integration with iNaturalist where suitable and develop data-sharing arrangements with projects such as Phenobase. These platforms can apply more advanced processing methods, including computer vision and neural networks, to the records we help collect.

3. Impacts

We hope to increase the number of growers contributing to phenology research, especially in regions where continuous crop observations are scarce.

Regular monitoring of identified plants should provide more useful information about the timing of developmental changes than isolated reports. Consistent photographic documentation and access to original observations will also allow external researchers to check results and apply new analytical methods without repeating the initial fieldwork.

By maintaining the connection between crop development and the conditions under which it was observed, our records may support more detailed investigations of agricultural responses to changing climates.

Our role is primarily to improve the collection of field data and make it available to researchers. We want existing phenology projects to benefit from observations that would otherwise remain undocumented, while ensuring that the growers who contribute them can also access and use the resulting information.

4. Scope

Crops40 crops in the first list: temperate fruit (apple, Asian pear, peach, ume, grape, persimmon, jujube…), subtropical and tropical fruit (citrus, lychee, longan, mango, durian…), cereals (rice, wheat, barley, maize, millet), legumes and oilseeds, vegetables and root crops.
Countries18 target countries: Korea, Japan, China, Taiwan, Mongolia, Viet Nam, Thailand, Lao PDR, Cambodia, Myanmar, Malaysia, Indonesia, Philippines, India, Bangladesh, Nepal, Bhutan, Sri Lanka. The pilot starts in Korea.
ObserversFarmers first, with support from extension services, farmer cooperatives, agricultural schools and research institutes.
StagesA small set of core stages per crop type that farmers can recognise without instruments.

7. How to join

Farmers and farmer groupsRead the guideline, choose your trees or plot, and start recording with the recording sheet or the online form.
Extension services, cooperativesOrganise a local farmer group and act as group leader. We provide training material.
Universities and research institutesBecome a national node: coordinate observers, review translations, check data quality.
TranslatorsTranslate the guideline into your language. See translation status.
DevelopersThe website is open source. See the repository.
